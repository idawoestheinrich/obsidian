In the simulation, you have a point-shaped light source that shines vertically onto the WOM from above. As the light source collimates to the maximum, the y-distance between the light source and the tube does not matter as long as it is outside the tube. The source shines in the centre of the tube, i.e. x=0. As a result, all remitted photons emanate from the same point. Depending on the z-distance between the light source and the PMT, there is a higher concentration of photons at the end of the tube, as most photons over a short distance do not manage to travel far enough around the tube. As a result, most of the photons at small distances are at the top. In the appendix is also a plot of the xy distribution at the end of the tube at a small distance (without the propagation through the window). However, the tube diameter is different (115mm outside, 110mm inside), but the principle remains the same. With a larger distance, the photons are distributed more evenly, but small asymmetries are also amplified by the window propagation, so that at 3.5mm you see a non point-symmetric distribution, while at 0.15mm everything appears to be symmetric. I could also imagine something similar happening with the angular distribution: When you are close to the end, the detected photons are dominated by direct hits, i.e. photons that reach the end without being totally reflected. The further away you go, the smaller this proportion becomes, which also reduces the angular distribution.
This should also result in a change in the correlation between xy coordinate and angle. However, I have not yet investigated these things quantitatively. I was thinking about investigating it systematically for my thesis at some point. If I find out anything, I'll let you know.
This could also explain your latest question: Firstly, you look at the photon distribution on the cathode. The distribution at the end of the tube looks different. This means that the photons do not ‘miss’ the tube, but the PMT does not see any light at the small point at the bottom where it couples to the tube. However, the PMT should still detect this light, provided it covers the entire tube and a little more. The reason for this could be that photons that leave the tube at the bottom must have significantly flatter angles than the photons at the top, as they have to rotate 180° in the tube within the 20cm distance. Due to the flatter angular distribution, the photons within the 3.5mm window tend to fly away from the coupling point. As there are hardly any photons with steep angles (almost perpendicular to the xy plane), the spot remains white. For the same reason you see photons ONLY at the coupling point and nothing above or below it. Most of the photons above are direct hits, and therefore have very steep angles, so they don't get far away from the original coupling site within the 3.5 mm.
As we only read out our WOMs with a large PMT, we cannot check the distributions from the simulation directly. But that would be possible with a SiPM board. I also know that a bachelor student from the ShiP group in Mainz did this once and was able to observe how the distribution changes depending on the azimuth angle of the light source. However, he did not correlate it with the simulation in his work as far as I know.
The simulation itself only propagates the photons to the end of the tube. Everything after that you have to calculate yourself. In the distributions that I have shown, I have not taken any coupling losses into account, so every photon that comes out at the end of the tube also reaches the cathode.
