## The Phenomena of Luminescence in Organic Materials

The underlying process of scintillation and the light guide technique with wavelength shifters used here is luminescence.
Luminescence in organic materials is the emission of photons with a characteristic spectrum from an excited molecule during the transition to its ground state S₀. The excited state is generated by externally supplied energy. This energy can be ionising radiation, as in scintillation, but also, for example, photons from the low-energy UV or visible spectrum (λ > 250 nm) as in photoluminescence (e.g., for wavelength shifters, see below). Luminescence encompasses various emission processes, including fluorescence and phosphorescence. These processes can be used to characterise materials and detect minimum ionising particles, depositing energy within the detector medium.
Fluorescence is the emission of light during the relaxation from an excited singlet state S₁ to the ground state S₀. An electron in the excited singlet state S₁ is paired with the second electron in the ground state-orbital. Since they have opposite spin, the return to the ground state is spin-allowed and occurs within nanoseconds through the emission of a photon. Fluorescence is the most important transition for fast scintillation signals. A physical system in which fluorescence occurs is called a fluorophore. If this fluorescence results from excitation by photons, it is called a photofluorophore [[Kolanoski 2016]].
In the excited triplet state T₁, the electron has the same spin orientation as the ground state electron. Hence, transitions to the ground state are suppressed, which makes the triplet state metastable. The relaxation from T₁ to the ground state by emitting a photon is called phosphorescence and typically occurs within milliseconds to seconds [[2006 Fluorescence]].

The processes that explain the absorption and emission of light through transitions between electronic and vibrational states on a molecular level are typically illustrated by Jablonski diagrams. 
A typical Jablonski diagram of an organic molecule shows the singlet ground state S₀, the first S₁, and second S₂ electronic states as well as the first triplet state T₁. Each electronic energy level can be divided into sub-levels corresponding to different vibration states (0, 1, 2, etc.). The distance between S₀ and S₁ is in the range of about 3–4 eV, which corresponds to a wavelength of about 400–300 nm. In contrast, the distance between the vibrational states of each electronic level is at least an order of magnitude smaller.
Following energy absorption, a fluorophore is usually excited to some higher vibrational level of either S₁ or S₂. These levels quickly relax to the lowest vibrational level of S₁ without radiation. This process is called *internal conversion* and generally occurs in the picosecond range, much faster than the relaxation to the ground state S₀. Hence, fluorescence emission generally results from the lowest-energy vibrational state of S₁ and typically to different excited vibrational levels of S₀. The rapid transition is to the lowest vibrational level S₁, and the radiant decay to higher vibrational levels of S₀ leads to an emitted photon, whose energy is generally lower than the absorbed energy. This change in the energy between absorption and emission is called a Stokes shift. However, since the two spectra overlap, there is always a certain probability that an emitted photon will be re-absorbed.
![[THEO_StokesShift.png]]
Photofluorophors with a large Stokes shift are called *wavelength-shifting* (WLS). They are, for example, used to absorb scintillation photons in the UV range and shift them to the visible range, which is detectable by conventional photomultipliers.
Since the remission of the photons is isotropic, wavelength-shifters are also used for light collection and guidance in the form of WLS fibres and WLS optical Modules.
As another result of the decay from the lowest vibrational level of S₁ to higher vibrational levels of S₀, the emission spectrum is typically a mirror image of the absorption spectrum. That is because the spacing of the vibrational levels of the excited states (S₁, S₂) is similar to that of the ground state S₀.
Transitions between singlet and triplet states are spin-forbidden for radiation. A fraction, however, can undergo a spin conversion to the first triplet state T₁ by non-radiating transitions called intersystem crossings (ISC) [[2006 Fluorescence]].
Phosphorescence photons from the relaxation from T₁ to the ground state S₀ are shifted to higher wavelengths relative to the fluorescence photons.
During the population of the T₁ level, transitions back to the exited singlet states can occur through further energy absorption. This can lead to delayed fluorescence (µs to ms).
In particle detectors, phosphorescence and delayed fluorescence should be avoided due to their long lifetime leading to large time delays, but also because the molecules in the long-living triplet states may be more susceptible to photobleaching [[Kolanoski, 2016]].
