### Light Collection

The escape through the scintillator boundaries and the absorption by the scintillator material are the two main processes that cause the loss of light from a scintillator. The absorption is only relevant when the total distance travelled by the photons is comparable to the attenuation length of the material.

The [[attenuation length]] $l$ is defined as the length after which the light flux is reduced by a factor of $e^{-1}$. The light flux $\Phi(x)$ as a function of travelled distance $x$ is commonly described by the [Beer-Lambert law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer–Lambert_law) and is given by:
$$
\Phi(x) = \Phi_0 e^{\frac{-x}{l}},
$$
where $\Phi_0$ is the initial light flux. 

The [[attenuation length]] also depends on the wavelength of the incoming light, as different mechanisms contribute to the absorption [[1994WRLeo]].

The largest contribution to the light loss of a scintillator is the escape through its boundaries. Since the scintillation emittance is isotropic, most photons reach the surfaces of the material before being detected or absorbed. The light will be partially transmitted or reflected depending on the angle of incidence of the light arriving at the surface.
