### Working Principle of a PMT

A photomultiplier is a device that converts incoming photons into measurable electrical signals based on the photoelectric effect. A schematic view of a [[PMT]] can be seen in 
![[THEO_PMT_Functionality.png]]
When a photon traverses the faceplate of the [[PMT]], it strikes the photocathode, liberating an electron through the photoelectric effect. The electron is accelerated and multiplied in the electron multiplier system. The electrons that hit the anode can be detected by a pulse-like decrease in voltage on the stem pins](THEO_PMT_Functionality)

It consists of an input window and a photocathode followed by a focusing electrode, electron multipliers, and anode to take the final signal. High voltage is applied to all these parts in an evacuated glass vessel during operation. The photocathode is a photosensitive material that covers the entrance window of the [[PMT]]. When a photon traverses the faceplate of the [[PMT]], it strikes the photocathode, liberating an electron through the photoelectric effect. The electron is then focussed and accelerated to the first electrode of the multiplier system.

The multiplier system is made of several metal electrodes called dynodes. These dynodes are arranged in stages, each at a progressively higher voltage potential. The purpose of the dynodes is to multiply the electrons emitted from the photocathode [[1994WRLeo]]. 

When an electron hits the first dynode, it transfers some energy to the electrons in the dynode. This causes secondary electrons to be emitted, which are accelerated and hit the next dynode, where the effect is repeated. A cascade of electrons hit the anode, the last electrode at the end of the dynode chain. The anode is held at a positive voltage potential compared to the dynodes to attract and collect the electrons. The current generated by the electrons is then converted into a measurable electrical signal.

A voltage divider network is used to maintain the voltage differences between the dynodes. It ensures that each dynode has a higher voltage potential than the previous one. It is coordinated via the stem pin base at the bottom of the vacuum vessel. Dark counts in [[PMT]] arise from thermal electrons generated in the photocathode or dynodes and generate a background signal.

A **photomultiplier** is a device used to **convert incoming photons into**  **measureable electrical signals** based on the photoelectric effect.
The **photocathode** is a **photosensitive material** that covers the entrance window of the PMT. When a **photon enters** the PMT, it strikes the photocathode, liberating an electron through the **photoelectric effect**. The photocathodematerial in this case is **bialkali** owing to its **high spectral sensitivity for blue** **light** and **low dark current**. 
The **Quantum efficiency of bialkali** is shown in this figure. As you can see the **peak of about 21% is at 360-400 nm**. **
In **neutrino interactions with the energy < 10 MeV,** each PMT in Super-
Kamiokande is hit almost at a **single-photoelectron level**. To detect single-photoelectron light the photomultiplier tubes need to have a very **good time and** **spacial resolution**.***
Inside the PMT, there are several metal electrodes called dynodes. These dynodes are **arranged in stages,** with each stage being at a **progressively higher voltage** **potentia**l. The purpose of the dynodes is to amplify the electrons emitted from the photocathode.

Photoelectrons traversing from the **cathode surface to the first dynode** are deflected by **Earth's geomagnetic field**; For all of them to hit the first dynode, the **area of the dynode is chosen as large as possible**. Furthermore, **three types** **of focusing mesh plates** are added between the cathode and the first dynode. The collection efficiency for photoelectrons at **the first dynode** now is **over 70%**.
At the end of the dynode chain, there is a **final electrode called the anode**. The anode is held at a positive voltage potential compared to the **dynodes to attract** **and collect the electrons**. The **current generated** by the electrons is then **converted into a measurable electrical signal.**
To **maintain the voltage differences** between the dynodes, a **voltage divider** **network** is used. It ensures that each dynode is at a higher voltage potential than the previous one.

Each PMT receives a **high voltage of approximately 2000 volts,** which leads to **an anode current that is 10 to 7 times higher than the photocathode current**.
