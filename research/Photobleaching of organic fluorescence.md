# Photobleaching of Organic Fluorophores
Various processes can lead to a decrease in the fluorescence intensity of a fluorophore. Reversible non-emissive decays to the ground state, which occur during the lifetime of the excited state, are called quenching. An example of quenching is the energy transfer from the excited molecules to other molecules. If these molecules are removed, the fluorescence intensity increases again. 

Some dyes also show fluctuations between fluorescent and non-fluorescent states on the second-to-minute timescale. These fluctuations are also reversible and are usually referred to as blinking.

However, high excitation intensities or chemical changes, such as oxidation, can also lead to irreversible loss of original optical properties in the fluorophore. These processes are generally referred to as photobleaching and are discussed in the following section, primarily based on [[2020 Photobleaching]] and [[Eggeling, 1999]], where the effect is studied in more detail.

The excess energy generated during an electronic excitation leads to a higher chemical reactivity of the fluorescent material, which can result in photobleaching. Any dye molecule can generally be bleached after some excitations due to various photochemical reactions.

Some of the relevant parameters for these are not yet known or not determined in sufficient detail. Thus, they are still part of current research ([[2020 Photobleaching]], [[2009 Photobleaching]], and others). As the chemical reactions leading to photobleaching are highly sensitive to environmental conditions, it is challenging to compare different studies and make reliable predictions about the properties of a specific environment. 

However, the photodegradation of organic fluorophores is generally described by two primary pathways: the *oxygen-dependent* and the *oxygen-independent* way. 

The first pathway requires the presence of molecular oxygen, which reacts with the excited fluorophore to generate highly reactive oxygen species. These can then trigger the oxidation or reduction of the dye molecules, thus destroying their ability to fluoresce. 

Photobleaching in the absence of oxygen is referred to as *oxygen-independent* and can be investigated by removing oxygen from the experiment. The dye is destroyed by other photochemical processes triggered by the generation of reactive radical intermediates, which are formed during the photoionisation of the molecules ([[2020 Photobleaching]]).

As the experiment discussed here was conducted under ambient conditions, the measured photobleaching is approximated by the *oxygen-dependent* pathway.

In many cases, the most important oxygen-dependent process is the formation of singlet oxygen $^1O₂*$, which reacts rapidly with the organic dye. A schematic view of this formation process is shown in ![[THEO_Photobleaching_explained.png]].

Singlet oxygen ^1O₂* is a highly reactive form of oxygen and is generated by an energy transfer from the excited triplet fluorophore T₁ to the triplet ground state of oxygen ^3O₂. As mentioned above, the excited triplet state T₁ is created by an intersystem crossing (ISC) after the fluorophore has been excited to the first singlet state S₁.

For each absorption process, there is a certain fixed probability for the molecule to be bleached, defined by the quantum yield of photobleaching QY_b. It is given by the number of photobleached molecules divided by the total number of photons absorbed during the same time interval:
$$QY_b = \frac{\text{\#bleached molecules}}{\text{\#absorbed photons}}$$
The probability P for a molecule to survive an number Nₑₓ𝒸ᶦᵗᵃᵒⁿₛ of excitations before being bleached is
$$P(\text{survival of }N_{excitations}) = (1-\text{QY}_b)^n\cdot\text{QY}_b.$$
The expected number of survived excitations µ with $N_{excitations}$ ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3...} is equal to the mean of this geometric distribution:
$$\mu = \frac{1-\text{QY}_b}{\text{QY}_b}.$$
Unfortunately, it is not possible to determine the Quantum Yield QY\_b directly as the total number of absorbed photons cannot be measured directly and precisely. However, if the dye solution is illuminated, it is possible to measure a decrease in the dye concentration cₓ(t) with time t corresponding to the number of irreversibly photobleached molecules.

A commonly used approximation for the process of photobleaching is a quasi-unimolecular reaction of the fluorophore X, which dissolves into an irreversibly photobleached molecule $X\_{bleached}$, activated by a photon with energy γ:
$$X + \gamma \xrightarrow{k_P} X_\text{bleached}.$$
Assuming a constant photon flux density φ, the decrease in the concentration of fluorescent molecules cₓ(t) can be described as a pseudo-first-order reaction:
$$c_X(t) = c_X(0) \exp \left( -k_P (\phi) t \right) = c_X(0) \exp \left( -k_P^* t \right)$$
where the initial concentration cₓ(0) at time t = 0 and the effective pseudo-first-order bleaching rate constant k\_P\* = k\_P ⋅ φ. By dividing k\_P\* by the photon flux density φ, a second-order rate constant k\_P can be obtained [[C6CP06015C]].
