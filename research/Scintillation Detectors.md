## Scintillation Detectors

Measuring ionising radiation using scintillation light is one of the oldest techniques for particle detection. As early as 1910, the physicist Rutherford detected α-particles by documenting the flashes of light they produced on a phosphorescent screen [[Kolanoski, 2016]]

[[Scintillator]] research has become increasingly active over the last three decades. Despite advances in semiconductor detectors, scintillators and PMTs are now more widely used than ever before. This results from the success in designing and developing new [[scintillator]] materials whose properties are well suited to the requirements of important applications such as medical imaging and high-energy physics [[Melcher, 2005]].

Both experiments discussed in this thesis are based on scintillation detectors. Scintillation is the generation of luminescence in a material following the absorption of ionising radiation. Ionising radiation is electromagnetic radiation or particles able to remove electrons from molecules and thus ionise them.

A scintillation detector consists of a [[scintillator]], which generates photons in the UV or visible range in response to incident radiation, a light guide, which enables direct coupling to a sensitive photon detector, that converts the light into an electrical signal, and electronics for processing this signal.

Scintillators are divided into inorganic crystals and organic (carbon-based) materials. Inorganic scintillators generate luminescence from electron-hole recombination in the crystal lattice. Organic scintillators generate luminescence through electron transitions from excited molecular states to the ground state. Dissolved in liquids, they can be polymerised into plastic scintillators of almost any geometric shape and are very flexible in application [[Kolanoski, 2016]]

While plastic scintillators provide signals with short rise and decay times in the ns range, both are typically longer for inorganic crystals. In particular, crystals with a high light yield (O(40,000) photons per MeV) often have decay times in the range of several hundred nanoseconds to microseconds. 

Due to their short decay times, scalability, and excellent handling properties, plastic scintillators are frequently used in particle and nuclear physics, as in the [[SHiP]] SBT subsystem, and the CheapCal prototype.
